SYNSheild Presentation Script (Aligned to SYNSheild (4).pdf)

Goal:
- Confident, technical, honest tone
- Smooth transitions from pitch framing to research validation
- Clear judge-focused narrative: value -> evidence -> deployability -> transparency
- Expand every abbreviation at first mention (then use the short form afterward)

Suggested pacing:
- Total: 12 to 15 minutes
- Slides 1-10 (framing): ~4 to 5 min
- Slides 11-23 (research core): ~7 to 8 min
- Slides 24-25 (close): ~1 to 2 min

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 1 - Title

Good [morning/afternoon], respected judges. I am Abed Al Rida Nehme, speaking for Team SYNSheild

today because displacement from the war has made travel impossible; we carry his work forward in

system for early SYN-flood DDoS detection, designed for performance, reproducibility, and practical
deployment.
Transition:
"Before technical details, let us briefly show how we will structure the presentation."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 2 - What We'll Cover
Talk track:
We split this presentation into two parts. Part A is practical framing: why this problem matters
and what value our system creates. Part B is research evidence: architecture, methodology,
evaluation, and deployment realism.
Transition:
"Now, let us look at a realistic first-minute attack timeline."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 3 - Hook Scenario (First minute)
Talk track:
To ground this in impact: during a Distributed Denial of Service, or DDoS, event, users may lose
access to core services, response times spike, and operations teams are forced into incident mode.
In the first minute of a SYN flood, risk escalates quickly. SYN means synchronize, the TCP handshake
flag used to initiate a connection. Think of the server like a restaurant with 100 seats: attackers keep
making fake reservations, so legitimate customers cannot sit. If we rely only on signatures and static 
thresholds, detection canbe noisy, delayed, and brittle under traffic variation. 
This is the operational gap we start from.
Transition:
"Now we revisit the same timeline and introduce the SYNSheild path."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 - Hook continuation / SYNSheild path
Talk track:
This is the same timeline, now with SYNSheild added. We process 0.3-second windows, convert them
to images, and generate a traceable model score. The key difference is speed plus auditability.
Transition:
"So what is the practical difference for operators and teams?"

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 - Without vs With SYNSheild
Talk track:
Without SYNSheild, teams rely on brittle rule behavior and often incomplete evaluation practices.
With SYNSheild, we provide end-to-end traceability, fair metrics such as macro-F1, and blind-test
evidence in a Huawei-aligned stack.
Transition:
"Next, we frame the background and scope as a formal research problem."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 - Background, Motivation, Scope
Talk track:
Protocol-level abuse, especially SYN flood, remains operationally relevant. As shown in the graph,
SYN-flood Distributed Denial of Service attacks are among the most common and dangerous attack
types in real traffic. Our motivation is earlier and more reliable Security Operations Center, or SOC,
support. Scope is intentionally focused: binary SYN-flood detection from traffic-window images.
Multi-attack automation is future work.
Transition:
"Given this scope, here is the exact problem we solve."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 - Problem
Talk track:
Our objective is early and reliable detection under real constraints: class imbalance, domain shift,
and latency/deployment limits. This matters because availability impacts education, business,
and public services. For example, a publicly reported CNCERT case described a large-scale DDoS
event affecting Chinese network resources with traffic peaking above 800 Gbps, showing how fast
operational impact can escalate.
Transition:
"Now we summarize our solution pipeline in one view."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 - Solution Snapshot
Talk track:
Pipeline in five steps: traffic input, SYN-oriented preprocessing and 0.3-second windowing,
grayscale image encoding, ResNeXt-50 inference in MindSpore, and SOC output with confidence logs.
Every stage is scriptable and auditable.
Transition:
"What makes this different from a generic Convolutional Neural Network, or CNN, demo?"

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 9 - Innovation Highlights
Talk track:
Our differentiation is the combination: ResNeXt-50 capacity, short-window representation,
imbalance-aware checkpointing using macro-F1, and an automated full pipeline from data preparation
to blind and cross-domain checks.
Transition:
"From here, we shift from value framing to formal research validation."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 - Transition to Part B
Talk track:
The next section follows research logic: related work, objectives, methods, experiments,
and reproducibility evidence.
Transition:
"We start by identifying where existing methods still fall short."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 11 - Related Work and Gap
Talk track:
Rule systems are fast but brittle, handcrafted-feature ML is explainable but engineering-heavy,
and image-CNN literature is promising but often lacks robust blind evaluation and reproducibility.
Our work specifically addresses these gaps.
Transition:
"Based on that gap, we define our research objectives."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 12 - Objectives and Hypothesis
Talk track:
Aim: a deployable and reproducible early SYN-flood detector. Objectives: robust image representation,
fair MindSpore evaluation, and blind/cross-domain validation. Hypothesis: 0.3-second windows with
ResNeXt improve practical early detection quality.
Transition:
"Now we show the system architecture."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 13 - System Model and Architecture
Talk track:
This pipeline is deterministic and modular: traffic input, labeling for testing, image generation,
inference, and alerting. Modular stages improve isolation, debugging, and reproducibility.
Transition (speaker handoff to Mohammad Farhat):
"I will now hand the floor to my colleague Mohammad Farhat, who will continue the presentation
from the data and methodology onward."
------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 14 - Data and Methodology
Talk track (Mohammad Farhat):
Thank you, Abed. From here I continue with our data path and methodology. We use supervised binary
classification on CICDDoS2019-derived Packet Capture, or PCAP, and ZIP
sources with train/val/test and blind sampling. Preprocessing is SYN-oriented and standardized to
224x224 grayscale windows. Model is ResNeXt-50 with macro-F1-focused training policy.
Transition:
"Then we define evaluation and protocol rigor."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 15 - Evaluation Criteria and Setup
Talk track:
Primary metrics are accuracy and macro-F1; macro-F1 is essential under class imbalance. We also use
Receiver Operating Characteristic, or ROC, and Precision-Recall, or PR, curves and a strict protocol
with train/val/test separation, balanced blind set, and macro-F1 checkpoint selection.
Transition:
"Now the implementation environment and reproducibility constraints."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 16 - Environment and Requirements
Talk track:
Training was validated on RTX 3070-class Graphics Processing Unit, or GPU, hardware with Python 3.10,
CUDA-compatible stack, MindSpore 2.6.0, and mindcv 0.3.0. The critical rule is representation
consistency: train-time and deploy-time preprocessing must match.
Transition:
"Next, visual intuition for what the model sees."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 17 - Sample Input Windows
Talk track:
Normal and DDoS windows create visibly different textures. This representation is useful because it
exposes attack structure in a way CNNs can learn effectively.
Transition:
"Now the headline performance results."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 18 - Performance Results
Talk track:
Our primary 0.3-second checkpoint achieves 98.21% validation accuracy with macro-F1 0.9399,
98.10% test accuracy with macro-F1 0.9375, and 98.9% on a 270-sample balanced blind set
with macro-F1 0.9889.
Transition:
"We now support those numbers with artifact-level evidence."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 19 - Proof Artifacts
Talk track:
This slide shows ROC, PR, confusion matrix, and calibration evidence, plus reproducibility commands
and logs. The point is transparency: results can be traced and reproduced, not just presented.
Transition:
"How does this compare to other method families?"

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 20 - Comparative Analysis
Talk track:
The key distinction is not only input representation. Our advantage is architecture depth plus fair
evaluation plus full pipeline reproducibility. Rule-based and handcrafted approaches remain useful
baselines but have known limitations under variation.
Transition:
"Now what these findings mean technically for real operations."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 21 - Discussion and Implications
Talk track:
Strong macro-F1 and blind performance indicate useful separation quality for early warning.
Operationally, this supports SOC workflows, while threshold tuning and adaptation for new domains
remain necessary.
Transition:
"Next, we connect this directly to Huawei ecosystem deployment."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 22 - Huawei Ecosystem Fit and Deployment
Talk track:
Our stack aligns with MindSpore and mindcv. Practical deployment pattern is router/switch telemetry
plus nearby edge/server inference, then SOC alerting with confidence logs. We explicitly avoid
unrealistic claims like CNN inference on every switch application-specific integrated circuit, or ASIC.
Transition:
"The next slide expands this into architecture-level deployment."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 23 - Practical Deployment Pattern
Talk track:
This architecture emphasizes realistic integration: telemetry export, nearby inference node, and SOC
integration. It is deployment-oriented while remaining consistent with model assumptions.
Transition:
"Finally, we close with transparency and next steps."

------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 24 - Honesty, Limitations, Roadmap
Talk track:
We openly acknowledge constraints: limited GPU availability in some environments and current focus
on SYN-flood class. Mitigation is clear, and roadmap is staged: near-term latency/threshold tuning,
mid-term multi-attack expansion, long-term cloud-edge orchestration. If we advance to the finals, we
see strong room to extend this work—for example a real Security Operations Center-style web portal
with live dashboards and push or email notifications for high-confidence alerts—so judges can see
the system end-to-end as an operator would.
Transition:
"Next, a short live demo using only logs from our deployed inference server, then we summarize and close."
